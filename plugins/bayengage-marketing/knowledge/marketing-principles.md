# Marketing Principles

The economics every skill in this package reasons from.

## Revenue decomposes

```
Revenue = Customers × Purchase Frequency × Average Order Value
```

Every marketing objective is an attack on one of those three terms, or on the retention that keeps the
first one from leaking. Naming which term an initiative targets prevents the most common planning
failure: a calendar full of activity that moves nothing in particular.

- More customers → acquisition, reactivation, win-back
- More frequency → replenishment, cross-sell, lifecycle automation, cadence
- Higher AOV → bundling, upsell, thresholds, premium positioning

## Retention compounds; acquisition does not

An acquired customer is a one-time gain. A retained customer is an annuity. For an established store,
a small improvement in repeat rate usually outperforms a large improvement in acquisition, because it
applies to a base that already exists and costs nothing to reach.

This is why this package weights retention, win-back and lifecycle work heavily, and why
[../rules/global-rules.md#G1](../rules/global-rules.md) puts customer value above engagement metrics.

## Existing customers are not one audience

The difference between a first-time buyer and a fourth-time buyer is larger than the difference between
most demographic segments. Behaviour beats attributes, and recency beats both.

## Attention is a finite, shared budget

Every send spends from the same account: the recipient's willingness to keep opening. Two campaigns
competing for the same week do not produce twice the revenue; they produce roughly the same revenue and
more unsubscribes. See [../rules/frequency-rules.md](../rules/frequency-rules.md).

## Discounting is buying a behaviour with margin

A discount is not free growth. It has a cost, it trains expectations, and a discount-trained audience
becomes progressively harder to sell to at full price. Before reaching for one, ask what behaviour is
being bought and whether a non-margin lever — access, timing, relevance, bundling, information — buys it
instead.

## Relevance outperforms volume, up to a point

Better targeting beats more sending, until the segments become too small to measure or maintain. The
useful question is not "can this be more targeted?" — it always can — but "does more targeting change
what we send?" See [segmentation-principles.md](segmentation-principles.md).

## Timing is often the whole intervention

The same message sent at the replenishment interval, at the decision moment, or at the individual's
engagement time can outperform a substantially better-written message sent arbitrarily. Timing is usually
the cheapest available improvement.

## Measure what you intended to change

If the objective was revenue, measure revenue. Opens and clicks are diagnostics that explain a result;
they are not the result. A campaign with a great open rate and no revenue is a failure with a good
excuse.

## Attribution is approximate — plan for that

No attribution model is correct. Use the platform's consistently, compare like with like, and avoid
decisions that only hold under one attribution window.

## Compounding beats heroics

A store that ships a competent plan every month and fixes its worst automation each quarter outperforms
one that runs a brilliant campaign twice a year. This package is biased toward durable systems —
automations, lifecycle coverage, repeatable calendars — over one-off brilliance.
