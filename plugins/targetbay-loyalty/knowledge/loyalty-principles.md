# Loyalty Principles

Durable reasoning about why loyalty programmes do and do not work. Skills cite this for *why*; the
decisions live in skills, and the constraints in [../rules/](../rules/README.md).

## The selection problem is the central difficulty

Members outspend non-members in essentially every store, and most of that gap existed before anyone
enrolled. Customers who already buy often are the ones who join. A programme evaluated on the raw
member/non-member difference will look excellent regardless of whether it does anything at all.

Everything useful about measuring a programme follows from taking this seriously: compare a member's
behaviour to their own behaviour before enrolment, or compare matched cohorts, or state plainly that the
effect is not isolated ([../rules/economics-rules.md#E9](../rules/economics-rules.md)).

## A programme changes behaviour at the margin, or not at all

The customer who was going to buy anyway earns points and costs margin. The programme's value is confined
to the decisions it actually alters: an extra purchase, a larger basket, a choice of this store over a
competitor, a return after a gap. Designing for the enthusiastic member is designing for the customer the
programme cannot influence.

## Points are debt

Issuance creates an obligation carried until redemption or expiry. That has two consequences most
programme discussions skip: the cost of a generous earn rate is incurred whether or not it changes
behaviour, and a programme with low redemption is accumulating obligation while delivering nothing
([../rules/economics-rules.md#E4](../rules/economics-rules.md)).

A rising balance with a flat redemption rate is the clearest sign a programme is failing, and it is
frequently reported as growth.

## Reachability decides participation

Members act on a reward they can picture reaching. The distance to the first meaningful reward — expressed
in orders, not in points — is the single most influential structural parameter, and it is derivable from
the store's own AOV and frequency rather than chosen
([../rules/economics-rules.md#E6](../rules/economics-rules.md)).

## Tiers work by naming a distinction that already exists

Where customers genuinely cluster into value groups, tiers make the distinction legible and give the
higher group something to protect. Where they do not, tiers invent a hierarchy members neither perceive
nor pursue. The value distribution comes first; the tier count follows from it
([../rules/tier-rules.md#T1](../rules/tier-rules.md)).

## Loss aversion is the strongest mechanic and the most dangerous

Members work harder to keep a tier than to reach one, which makes downgrade policy powerful. It is also
the mechanism by which a programme generates its worst customer experiences, because losing a status feels
like a penalty for a year of loyalty. Anything that takes away what a member holds needs deliberate design
rather than a default ([../rules/safety-rules.md#S2](../rules/safety-rules.md)).

## Complexity is paid for by the member

Every additional mechanic — multipliers, challenges, bonus events, badges — is something the member must
understand before it can influence them. A programme a customer cannot summarise in one sentence produces
less behaviour change than a simpler one, even when the simpler one is objectively less generous.

## Referral and loyalty are different products sharing a surface

Loyalty rewards existing behaviour; referral buys new customers. They are often built together because
they share a member identity, but their economics, their measurement and their failure modes have nothing
in common ([../rules/referral-rules.md#F1](../rules/referral-rules.md)).
