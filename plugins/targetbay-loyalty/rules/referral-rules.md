# Referral Rules

Constraints on referral programme design. Cited as `referral-rules.md#F2`.

---

### F1. A referral programme is an acquisition channel and is costed as one.
The incentive on both sides is the acquisition cost. Compare it against the store's other acquisition
costs and against the referred customer's observed value, not against the referrer's enthusiasm.

### F2. Reward the outcome, not the action.
Qualify the referral on a completed, retained order — past the return window where the store has one.
Rewarding a signup, a share or a click produces exactly the behaviour it pays for, and very little else.

### F3. State the incentive split and why it is split that way.
Two-sided incentives are not automatically correct. Decide what the referrer needs to act and what the
referred customer needs to convert, from this store's data, and state both.

### F4. Fraud controls are part of the design, not a follow-up.
Self-referral, address and payment-instrument collusion, and incentive farming are predictable. State the
controls at design time and what they cost in legitimate referrals blocked.

### F5. Never make the referral incentive better than the customer's own acquisition offer.
If referring is worth more than buying, members refer themselves through other means. Check the incentive
against existing acquisition offers before proposing it.

### F6. Measure referred-customer retention, not referral volume.
Referral programmes reliably produce volume and unreliably produce customers. Track the referred cohort's
repeat behaviour against other acquisition sources
([economics-rules.md#E9](economics-rules.md) applies to this comparison too).

### F7. Asking is part of the programme.
A referral programme nobody is told about performs like no programme. Where and when members are asked is
a design decision, and it is bound by the same consent and frequency constraints as any other messaging
([global-rules.md#G13](global-rules.md)).
